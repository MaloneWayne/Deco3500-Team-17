# Methods

HCI methods have to match the claim. Our claim is social, not statistical: **the split is painful because of face, not because people cannot add**.

## What we used

**Semi-structured interviews (n = 6).** Rize Ma asked the same five prompts each time: a time they paid for something they did not use; how the house settles now; what happens when consumption differs; whether chores ever stand in for cash; whether humour or a neutral tool ever lowered the heat.

We did not fish for campus stories. Participants were people in sharehouses (including a house with a partner, P04). Several study. The unit of analysis was the household.

Raw notes live in the repo: [`Interview Transcript 01.docx`](https://github.com/MaloneWayne/Deco3500-Team-17/blob/main/Ruize%20Ma/Interview%20Transcript%2001.docx).

**Framework analysis.** We ran the six social dimensions on the current direction: objective, mechanism, technology, people’s contribution, consequences, key assumption. That stopped us from pitching “an app that tracks spending” and forced us to say what social move the system makes.

**Social Possibility Wheel.** We diverged on *shared objects* and *awareness* before we converged. Shared objects became the receipt, the bounty card, the house pool. Awareness became the live claim list on everyone else’s phone, without forcing talk.

**Concept matrix.** Three concepts in the same four-colour split (overview, mechanism, contribution, consequences):

- Campus Thrift Radar (location)
- Budget Quest (shared objects)
- Cooling-off Buddy Alert (contextual push)

Interviews killed the first and the third. We kept the one that could be **played in a room**.

**Prototyping as inquiry.** A mid-fi web ritual (claim, orphan, veto, slider, bounty, lock) plus visual screens for the same loop. Building the swipe and the veto flash showed us where the design actually carries the conflict.

## What the interviews revealed that the frameworks could not

The frameworks told us *where* to look. The interviews told us *what people already do*.

- **Silence is the current “feature.”** P01, P02, P03, P05, P06 all described paying and swallowing it. P04 was the rare person who spoke up about protein powder, and they called it an accusation.
- **Tools start too late.** Splitwise, Messenger, WeChat, and bank transfers appear after someone has already decided the split. P04 said the hard work is classifying the item, not logging the dollar.
- **Equal is a social lubricant.** Houses split evenly because it is fast, then remember the unfairness anyway (P02’s extra $10 on takeaway; P05 arriving late to dinner).
- **Grey items, not totals.** Alcohol vs groceries (P02). Milk that three people drink (P04). Cleaning spray bought while P03 was away. The fight is classification.
- **Humour already mediates.** Rock-paper-scissors for toilet paper (P01). A joke about spinning a wheel for the air-con bill (P03). Random-choice apps (P04). Our spinner is not a gimmick we invented in a vacuum. It is a thing houses already reach for.
- **Anonymity needs a receipt of its own.** P06 wanted a vote that does not embarrass anyone, *and* a clear explanation of the result. Hide the person. Show the maths.

## Experience requirements we took from that

R1. Raise a concern without naming yourself.  
R2. Show how each total was made.  
R3. Allow more than one split method (equal, item, personal, proportional).  
R4. Show who has reviewed.  
R5. Keep confrontation off the human.  
R6. Let the house mark an item shared, personal, or in-between.  
R7. Non-money trades need a group yes.  
R8. Games can cool the room; they must not punish the broke housemate.

## What we did not do (yet)

No diary study. No kitchen observation of a live split. No pretend extra quotes.

When we test next, the method should be a **colocated session** with a real receipt, plus a short interview after about whether anyone felt they had to speak. That matches the claim.

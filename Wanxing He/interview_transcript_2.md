# Interview Transcript 2

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a specific time when a shared house bill, like a grocery run or group takeout, included items you didn't consume or barely used, but you ended up paying an equal share anyway. Why did you choose not to speak up at the time, and how did that decision affect the dynamic in the house afterward?

**Participant:** Yeah, I remember one time we did a big grocery shop together. There were some snacks and frozen food that I don't really eat, but we still split everything equally.

I thought about saying something, but everyone was already sending the money and I didn't want to be the difficult person over a few dollars.

After that, I started buying more of my own food. I was still friendly with everyone, but I trusted the shared shopping system less.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the exact routine your house currently uses to calculate and request money for shared expenses. Do you ever sit in the same room when the math is being done and the requests are sent, or does it happen entirely over text or push notifications? Where does that current routine feel the most awkward or stressful?

**Participant:** In our house, whoever pays usually takes a photo of the receipt and puts it in the group chat.

Then they work out the split and send their bank details or a payment request. We almost never do it together in person.

The stressful part is when the total is higher than I expected. Sometimes the message just appears like, “You owe 45 dollars,” and I'm like, “For what exactly?”

I normally check the receipt first before paying.

**Interviewer:** Think of a time when your house bought something to share, like a bulk grocery order, a large takeout meal, or shared drinks, but people consumed vastly different amounts. How did the group decide whether to split the cost evenly or proportionally, and how did you navigate the conversation if someone felt they were paying for more than they actually consumed?

**Participant:** We had this issue with drinks for a house party.

Some people drank quite a lot, and two of us barely drank anything. At first, someone suggested splitting the whole cost equally.

I said I didn't think that made sense, because I only had one drink. We ended up splitting the food evenly, but people paid separately for the drinks they had.

It was a little awkward for maybe one minute, but after we agreed on it, it was fine.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe a situation where someone in the house was short on cash for a shared expense, so they compensated by taking on extra household duties, like buying the next round, doing a deep clean, or cooking? How did the house negotiate and agree on the value of that favor?

**Participant:** One of my housemates was waiting for their pay to come in, so they couldn't pay for some shared groceries that week.

They said they would cook dinner for everyone twice and clean the kitchen.

We didn't really put a dollar value on it. It was more like, “Okay, you help out more this week and that's fine.”

Nobody had a problem with it because it was only temporary.

**Interviewer:** Describe a time when a tense conversation about household money, chores, or responsibilities was successfully diffused by humor, a quick game like rock-paper-scissors or a coin flip, or an external mediator. How did shifting the focus away from a direct argument change the mood in the room?

**Participant:** We had an argument about whose turn it was to clean the bathroom.

Nobody could remember the cleaning schedule, and everyone was blaming someone else.

One of my housemates just opened a random number app and said the person with the lowest number had to do it.

We all laughed and agreed to it.

It made the situation much less serious. Instead of arguing about who was right, we just accepted the result and moved on.

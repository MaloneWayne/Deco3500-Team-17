**Interviewer:** Tell me about a specific time when a shared house bill, like a grocery run or group takeout, included items you didn't consume or barely used, but you ended up paying an equal share anyway. Why did you choose not to speak up at the time, and how did that decision affect the dynamic in the house afterward?

**Participant:** Yeah, there was one time we ordered takeaway for everyone. A few people added desserts and extra sides, but I just got a basic meal.

When we split the bill, it was divided evenly. I noticed I was paying more than what I actually ordered, but I didn't say anything.

I think I just didn't want to make the whole thing complicated. It was only a small difference.

After that, I started telling people exactly what I ordered before we paid, just to avoid the same situation.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the exact routine your house currently uses to calculate and request money for shared expenses. Do you ever sit in the same room when the math is being done and the requests are sent, or does it happen entirely over text or push notifications? Where does that current routine feel the most awkward or stressful?

**Participant:** Most of the time, one person pays and then sends a message in the house group chat.

They usually write the total and how much each person needs to send. Sometimes they include a photo of the receipt too.

We don't really talk about it face-to-face unless someone has a question.

The awkward part is probably asking someone to pay when they haven't paid for a few days. No one really wants to keep reminding people.

**Interviewer:** Think of a time when your house bought something to share, like a bulk grocery order, a large takeout meal, or shared drinks, but people consumed vastly different amounts. How did the group decide whether to split the cost evenly or proportionally, and how did you navigate the conversation if someone felt they were paying for more than they actually consumed?

**Participant:** We had this with a big barbecue once.

Some people ate a lot more meat, and some people mostly had salad and snacks. At first, we were going to split everything evenly.

One person said that didn't feel very fair, so we talked about it.

In the end, we split the basic food evenly, but the people who wanted the more expensive stuff paid a little extra.

It wasn't perfect, but everyone seemed okay with it.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe a situation where someone in the house was short on cash for a shared expense, so they compensated by taking on extra household duties, like buying the next round, doing a deep clean, or cooking? How did the house negotiate and agree on the value of that favor?

**Participant:** Yeah, one of my housemates couldn't pay their part of a shared dinner one week.

They said they would cook for everyone the next weekend and do the grocery run.

We didn't really calculate the value of it. It was more casual than that.

We just agreed because it felt fair enough, and no one wanted to make it too serious.

**Interviewer:** Describe a time when a tense conversation about household money, chores, or responsibilities was successfully diffused by humor, a quick game like rock-paper-scissors or a coin flip, or an external mediator. How did shifting the focus away from a direct argument change the mood in the room?

**Participant:** We had a small disagreement about who had to clean the kitchen after a party.

Everyone said they had already done enough, so it was starting to get a bit annoying.

Someone suggested flipping a coin between the two people who were left.

We did that, and everyone started joking about it.

It made the situation feel less personal. The person who lost still wasn't very happy, but at least the argument stopped.

# **Interview 2:** 

- **Participant Profile:** Iris, 24, Master of Interaction Design international student at UQ. 

- **Living Situation:** 4-person house in St Lucia, living with three other postgraduate international students. They coordinate bulk grocery trips, cook communal batch meals, split utility bills, order shared deliveries, and go out to cafes and pubs on weekends. 

**Interviewer:** Let's start with the first scenario. Tell me about a time when a shared house bill included items you didn't consume, but you paid an equal split anyway. Why did you stay quiet, and what was the ripple effect? 

**Iris:** Last month, our house went to an international supermarket in Sunnybank to stock up on bulk spices, rice, and pantry goods. While we were there, two of my housemates loaded up on artisanal sauces, specialty snacks, and expensive packs of instant noodles that only they eat. When we checked out, the total was over $260. The girl who paid tapped her card, divided the receipt by four in our group chat, and asked for $65 each. 

**Interviewer:** What stopped you from requesting an itemized split? 

**Iris:** We are all postgrads with heavy research deadlines. Everyone was exhausted, carrying heavy boxes to the car in the heat. Stopping the group to manually tick off twenty individual line items on a foot-long paper receipt felt pedantic and socially exhausting. I didn't want to be perceived as petty over $15 or $20. But the dynamic shifted; over the next two weeks, I stopped offering to share the snacks I personally bought. I kept my own groceries inside my bedroom cupboard instead of the communal pantry to protect my own budget. 

**Interviewer:** Walk me through your household's exact settlement ritual. Do you sit together to calculate expenses, or is it done digitally? Where does the routine feel most awkward? **Iris:** We have an asynchronous system centered around a shared WhatsApp group and an overdue Splitwise balance. Usually, one housemate tallies everything up on a Sunday night while sitting alone in their room, then drops a message in the group: "Hey guys, updated Splitwise for the week, please settle when you can!" 

**Interviewer:** What makes that process uncomfortable? 

**Iris:** The asynchronous lag is agonizing. One person always pays within five minutes. Another person leaves the notification unread for four days. If you're the person who is owed $120, you have to see them in the kitchen making breakfast every morning, wondering, _Do they remember? Are they ignoring it, or are they waiting for payday?_ Sending a follow-up text feels like nagging, but saying it in person while they're cooking feels confrontational. That limbo creates unnecessary domestic friction. 

**Interviewer:** Can you recall an instance where the house bought something together, but consumption was disproportionate? How did you navigate deciding between an even split versus a proportional one? 

**Iris:** We ordered pizza and craft beer delivery for an assignment submission celebration. One housemate doesn't drink alcohol, and I only had two slices of vegetarian pizza. The other two housemates drank two six-packs between them and ate two meat-lovers pizzas and garlic bread. 

**Interviewer:** Did the group try to split it proportionally? 

**Iris:** It was an awkward conversation. The non-drinker directly spoke up and said, "I'm happy to chip in for pizza, but I shouldn't pay for the beer." That immediately made the atmosphere tense. The two who drank felt defensive, like they were being audited. They tried to manually subtract 

the alcohol, but then argued about how the $12 delivery fee and Uber service surcharge should be redistributed. In the end, we spent twenty minutes passing a phone around doing confusing arithmetic while the food got cold. It ruined the celebratory vibe. 

**Interviewer:** Has anyone in your house ever substituted chores or favors for money when cash was tight? How did the group decide on the value of that labor? 

**Iris:** Yes. One of our housemates had a delay in his overseas scholarship stipend and couldn't pay his $75 share of the quarterly internet and water bill. He has a car, so he offered to act as our dedicated driver for two weeks—taking us to campus, doing all the heavy grocery runs, and picking people up from late library sessions. 

**Interviewer:** How did the house establish whether that labor matched the $75 bill? **Iris:** We never set a real metric. We just said, "Yeah, sure, that helps a lot." But a week in, someone asked him for an airport pickup at 6 AM, and he felt taken advantage of, while the person asking felt it was justified because petrol and parking costs were high. It proved that without an explicit conversion rate or clear task scope, bartering financial debt for physical favors breeds confusion on both sides. 

**Interviewer:** Finally, tell me about a time when financial or household tension was diffused by humor, a game, or an objective third party or tool. How did it change the dynamic? **Iris:** We had an ongoing disagreement about who should pay for the communal olive oil, laundry detergent, and dish soap. The bottles were empty, and everyone claimed, "I barely used any of that detergent, I shouldn't have to buy the next 5-liter jug." The tension was tangible in the kitchen. 

**Interviewer:** How was that resolved? 

**Iris:** One housemate pulled out a deck of cards and placed four cards facedown on the dining table, saying the lowest card had to buy the supplies, but the next time, they'd be immune. We all drew a card. I drew a 3 of clubs, so I lost. But because it was completely up to chance and framed as a parlor game, everyone cheered, I laughed, and I bought the supplies that afternoon without feeling resentful. Leaving the outcome to an impartial, gamified mechanic completely neutralized the interpersonal blame.

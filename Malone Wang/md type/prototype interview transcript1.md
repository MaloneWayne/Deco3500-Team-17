### Interview 1: Undergraduate Student (3-Person Shared Apartment)

**Participant Profile:**

- **Participant:** 2nd-year undergraduate international student at the University of Queensland.
- **Living Context:** 3-bedroom apartment, shared with two fellow UQ undergraduate friends.
- **Household Dynamics:** Frequent joint Coles runs, shared nightly cooking, joint UberEats/DoorDash orders on weekends.

##### Feature 1: The Receipt "Draft"

(Interviewer hands smartphone displaying the The Receipt Draft screen showing "\$12.00 Fancy Truffle Cheese", with "Pass" and "Claim" buttons.)

**Interviewer:** Take a look at this screen without any explanation from me. What do you think is happening here, and what would you do next?

**Interviewee:** Okay, it looks like Tinder, but for groceries. It says "Sunday Grocery Run – Item 4 of 18," and there's a \$12 cheese. It shows "3 Housemates Claimed" with little avatar icons. I guess if I also ate this cheese, I hit the green "Claim" button, and if I didn't touch it, I hit the red "Pass" button.

**Interviewer:** Exactly right. In this system, all three flatmates sit down together in the living room on Sunday, and the receipt deals items out to everyone's phone simultaneously. If two of you claim it, it splits two ways; if only one claims it, they pay 100%. How would this dynamic feel in your living room?

**Interviewee:** It's super fast, and I love that it eliminates doing manual math on a paper receipt. But here's the real scenario: what about shared cooking basics? Say someone buys garlic or soy sauce. I might use a tiny splash, while my roommate uses half the bottle. Do I swipe "Claim" or "Pass"? If I swipe "Pass" on cooking oil that I used once, will the guy who bought it look across the coffee table and say, "Bro, you used that for your stir-fry yesterday"? Because we're sitting right next to each other, swiping "Pass" right in front of them might feel just as awkward as arguing verbally.

##### Feature 2: The "Icebreaker" Veto & The Review Phase

(Interviewer shows Pre-Lock Review, then transitions to Veto Played! and Friday Night Pizza Slider.)

**Interviewer:** Here is the Pre-Lock Review screen before the weekly balance is locked in. What do you think the "Veto Tokens" and "Play Veto Card" are doing?

**Interviewee:** It says "2 of 3 remaining this month" with shields. So I have limited chances to contest the bill. If I tap "Looks Fair," it finalizes. If something feels wrong, I tap "Play Veto Card.

**Interviewer:** Now imagine you tap "Play Veto Card." The app transitions to this screen on everyone's phones. (Interviewer displays the red Veto Played! screen with a 2-minute timer and "Table Talk Time".) What do you think happens in your living room when this pops up?

**Interviewee:** Wow, the red screen is aggressive! In a house of three people, "anonymous" is an illusion. If Flatmate A paid the bill, and Flatmate B says "I clicked Looks Fair," then everyone immediately knows it was me who dropped the nuke. The 2-minute "Table Talk" timer makes it feel like an emergency room triage or an _Among Us_ meeting. It definitely forces us to talk, but it might make a small disagreement feel like a major confrontation because of the dramatic visual sirens.

**Interviewer:** (Switches screen to Friday Night Pizza (\$80 Total) slider.) Here is how the app handles proportional food splits after a veto. You have a slider: "Just a Nibble (10%)," "Average (25%)," and "Feral (50%)". How do you feel about this?

**Interviewee:** That's brilliant—"Feral" is hilarious! We literally joke about this whenever we order Domino's. But here's the issue: what if the total percentages don't add up to 100%? If all three of us slide down to "Just a Nibble" to pay less, who covers the rest of the \$80? If you let people self-report consumption, people will naturally underestimate how much they ate unless someone calls them out.

##### Feature 3: The Barter & Penalty System (Chores for Debt)

(Interviewer displays House Bounty Proposal showing "Debt Offset Request: -\$10.00" for "Bin Duty & Recycling" for 2 weeks.)

**Interviewer:** What do you understand from this screen?

**Interviewee:** Housemate B owes \$10 for groceries and is offering to take out the red and yellow council bins and handle recycling for two weeks to clear that debt. The other housemates vote "Accept Trade" or "Decline Trade".

**Interviewer:** Would you personally accept this kind of chore trade in your apartment?

**Interviewee:** For \$10? Honestly, no. Bin duty takes two minutes on Tuesday night. Taking out the bins twice isn't worth \$10 of real grocery cash out of my pocket. Plus, what happens if we vote "Accept" and forgive his debt, but next Tuesday he forgets to put the bins on the curb and the council truck passes? The debt is already cleared in the app, but the rubbish is rotting in the sun. It introduces chore monitoring, which causes way more housemate fights than a \$10 split.

##### Feature 4: The "Orphan Item" Penalty Game

(Interviewer displays Unclaimed Item: \$14.00 Dishwasher Pods with the spinner wheel.)

**Interviewer:** What do you think happened to trigger this screen?

**Interviewee:** Nobody swiped claim on the \$14 dishwasher pods during the draft phase, so it became an orphan. Now there's a spinner wheel with everyone's names on it, and whoever it lands on absorbs the cost or has to do dishes.

**Interviewer:** How would you feel if you lost the spin and had to pay the full \$14 for pods that all three of you use to clean your bowls?

**Interviewee:** I would be furious! Dishwasher pods are communal. Why should one person get punished for buying an essential household item just because no single individual "owns" it? This turns a shared responsibility into Russian roulette. It encourages people to play chicken—everyone will intentionally swipe "Pass" on toilet paper, trash bags, and cleaning supplies hoping someone else gets stuck with it, or gamble on the spinner.

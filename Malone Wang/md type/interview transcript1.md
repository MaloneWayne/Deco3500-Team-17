# **Interview 1:** 

- **Participant Profile:** Leo, 20, 2nd-year Bachelor of Computer Science international student at UQ. 

- **Living Situation:** 3-person apartment in Toowong, living with two university classmates. They share basic pantry staples, take turns buying groceries at Coles and local Asian grocers, cook shared dinners on weeknights, and split weekend delivery orders. 

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a specific time when a shared house bill—like groceries or takeout— included items you didn't consume or barely touched, but you paid an equal share anyway. Why did you choose not to speak up, and how did that affect things afterward? 

**Leo:** Yeah, that happens way more often than I’d like. About three weeks ago, we went to Coles for our weekly restock. My roommate Marcus grabbed two six-packs of ginger beer and some premium beef cuts for himself. I’m vegetarian on weekdays for cultural and budget reasons, and I don't drink sugary sodas at all. When Marcus scanned the receipt through our shared tally, he just divided the whole $142 bill by three. It came to around $47 each. My actual items were maybe $22. 

**Interviewer:** Did you bring it up with him right then? 

**Leo:** No. In the moment, we were putting groceries into the fridge and chatting. If I stopped him and said, "Hey, deduct your $18 steaks and $14 drinks," it would look like I was being stingy over twenty bucks. As an international student, I already worry about seeming antisocial or difficult to live with. But honestly, it bothered me for days. The next time we cooked dinner, I noticed Marcus took the largest portion without asking, and I felt this quiet resentment boiling up. I didn't say anything, but I withdrew to my room earlier than usual that entire week. **Interviewer:** That makes total sense. Moving to our second question: walk me through the exact routine your house uses to calculate and request money for shared expenses. Do you sit together to do the math, or does it happen asynchronously over your phones? Where is the friction? 

**Leo:** It’s completely asynchronous, which actually makes it weirder. Marcus or our other flatmate, Sam, will take a photo of the paper receipt and upload the total onto Splitwise around 11 PM when we're all in our separate bedrooms. Then my phone pings with a notification: "Marcus added an expense: $47.33." 

**Interviewer:** What makes that routine feel stressful or awkward? 

**Leo:** It’s the silence attached to it. We could have been laughing on the couch five minutes earlier, but the moment the notification pops up in private, it feels like an invoice from a landlord. You wonder: _Do I need to PayID this second? If I wait until tomorrow morning, will they think I’m broke or dodging it?_ If there's an error on the receipt, contesting it via text feels cold and aggressive, so you just pay it to avoid creating digital drama. 

**Interviewer:** Think about a time when your flat bought something to share—like a bulk order or a big meal—where consumption was totally unequal. How did you determine whether to split evenly or proportionally, and how did that conversation go? 

**Leo:** We ordered a large Korean fried chicken set on DoorDash during midterms. Sam and Marcus ordered two whole bone-in chickens and extra sides, while I just had a small bowl of chips and a side of pickled radish because I wasn’t super hungry. When the bill arrived with service fees and delivery, it was nearly $90. Marcus said, "Let’s just do thirty each to keep the math easy." 

**Interviewer:** How did you handle that? 

**Leo:** I tried to make a joke: "Bro, I basically ate five fries and a radish slice!" Sam laughed and agreed I barely touched the meat, but neither of them offered a specific lower number. Because nobody wanted to do the manual percentage calculation with the delivery fee and promotional voucher accounted for, I said, "Fine, whatever, just split it three ways." It felt unfair because keeping the math simple always benefits the person who consumed the most. **Interviewer:** Has there ever been a situation where someone was short on cash and compensated with household labor instead, like deep cleaning or cooking? How did you agree on the value of that favor? 

**Leo:** Yes. Last semester, Sam had an unexpected car repair bill and was waiting on his family allowance. He couldn’t cover his $60 share of the monthly cleaning supplies and shared pantry items. He told us, "Look, I’ll deep-clean the kitchen, degrease the stove, and mop the entire apartment this weekend if you guys cover my share." Marcus and I agreed instantly because neither of us wanted to clean the range hood. 

**Interviewer:** Did the valuation feel fair to everyone? 

**Leo:** In theory, yes, but there was no standard rate. Sam spent three hours scrubbing everything, which is worth way more than $60 if you hired a cleaner. But two weeks later, when another expense came up, there was confusion over whether his "credit" had expired or if that one clean earned him leeway on smaller bills. Without clear boundaries, informal trades get muddy quickly. 

**Interviewer:** Describe a time when household tension around money or chores was successfully diffused by humor, a game, or an external tool. How did shifting focus away from a direct confrontation help? 

**Leo:** We had an ongoing argument about taking out the general waste and recycling bins down three flights of stairs to the complex collection area. Nobody wanted to do it, especially when a bag leaked. One Sunday night, the bin was overflowing, and Marcus and Sam were getting snippy about who took it out last. 

**Interviewer:** What broke the tension? 

**Leo:** Sam pulled out his phone and opened an online roulette wheel with our three names on it. He cast his screen to the living room TV and spun it. Marcus’s name came up with this obnoxious carnival sound effect. We all burst out laughing. Marcus groaned, threw his hands up, and took the bag down without complaining. Shifting the blame to a random digital tool completely removed the feeling of being targeted by your friends.

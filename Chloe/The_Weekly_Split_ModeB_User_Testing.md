## 1. Session Information

**Project:** Team 17 — The Weekly Split  
**Prototype:** Mid-fidelity mobile web prototype  
**Testing Mode:** Mode B — Housemates testing together  
**Number of Participants:** 4  
**Participant Codes:** P1, P2, P3, P4  
**NPC:** Off  
**Testing Device:** One mobile phone passed between participants  
**Estimated Session Duration:** 43 minutes  
**Facilitator:** Team 17 member  
**Session Type:** Moderated think-aloud usability testing  

### Participant Relationship

The four participants live together in the same share house and consist of two couples:

- **P1 and P2 — Couple A**
- **P3 and P4 — Couple B**

All four participants regularly share meals together.

Their usual routine is not to calculate every grocery purchase individually. Instead, the two couples generally alternate responsibility for food. For example, Couple A may purchase groceries and cook dinner one day, while Couple B does the same on another day.

However, grocery purchases sometimes contain personal items that are only intended for one participant, such as snacks, specialty coffee, protein products, or other personal-use products.

Therefore, their household uses a combination of:

- shared household spending;
- couple-level spending;
- personal spending; and
- informal balancing of costs over several days.

This household was selected because their existing behaviour provides a useful context for evaluating whether The Weekly Split can support complex shared-expense situations rather than only simple equal bill splitting.

## 3. Participant Profiles

### P1 — Female, Couple A

P1 is relatively budget-conscious and usually pays attention to whether shared expenses are distributed fairly.

She often remembers who paid for previous grocery trips and is the person most likely to notice when a personal item has accidentally been included in a shared bill.

She is comfortable discussing money with her partner but slightly less comfortable raising small financial issues with the other couple.

### P2 — Male, Couple A

P2 is less concerned about small differences in spending.

He usually helps with cooking and occasionally shops together with P1.

He prefers simple settlement methods and stated that he would rather ignore a difference of a few dollars than spend a long time calculating it.

### P3 — Female, Couple B

P3 frequently purchases groceries for both herself and P4.

She clearly distinguishes personal products from household products and is more likely to object if something that is obviously personal is divided equally.

She also pointed out that the person physically purchasing an item is not always the person who uses it.

### P4 — Male, Couple B

P4 regularly cooks and is also often the person who initially pays for takeaway or groceries.

He prefers fast settlement and normally focuses on the final amount rather than detailed calculations.

He is comfortable discussing money within the household but does not want financial settlement to become a long weekly activity.

## 5. Task Testing

## T1 — Sit Down and Start

### Task

Participants were told:

> “You are a housemate at this table. Pick who you are tonight, then start the settlement.”

Each participant selected one fictional seat:

- P1 — Alex
- P2 — Bailey
- P3 — Casey
- P4 — Drew

They then selected **Deal the Receipt**.

### Completion

- **Completed:** Yes
- **Hints required:** 0
- **Approximate completion time:** 42 seconds

### Observation

P1 briefly hesitated because she initially expected the names to represent the real participants.

P3 asked whether users would normally create their own profile before starting.

P2 and P4 immediately understood that the fictional names represented four household members.

No participant needed assistance locating the start button.

### T1q1. In your own words, what is this app for?

**P1**

**P4**

**Interpretation:**  
All participants understood the main purpose of the prototype without needing a tutorial.

### T1q2. Did anything on this first screen feel like a lecture?

**P1**

> “No. It was quite straightforward.”

**P2**

> “No.”

**P3**

> “No, but maybe one sentence explaining the steps would help.”

**P4**

> “No. The big button makes it obvious what to do.”

**Finding:**  
The entry screen successfully communicated the activity without overwhelming participants, although P3 wanted slightly clearer expectations about the overall process.

### Completion

- **Completed:** Yes

### Observed Behaviour

The four-person interaction was noticeably slower for ambiguous products than for obvious shared products.

The **Communal Basket** and **Chicken Thighs** were accepted by all participants very quickly.

The **Premium Protein** caused the first extended conversation.

P3 said:

> “This is where it depends on who it is actually for.”

P4 claimed the product.

The **Specialty Coffee** also produced discussion because P2 and P4 said they would use it, while P1 and P3 would not.

All four participants passed the **Heavy-Duty Cleaner** at first because none interpreted “Claim” as meaning “this is a household item everyone should share.”

This became important in the following task.

### T2q1. When three people claimed the cheese, what did you think the $4 each meant?

**P1**

> “Three people said they would use it, so twelve dollars is divided between those three people.”

**P2**

> “It means I don't pay because I passed, and the others pay four each.”

**P3**

> “That part is very clear.”

**P4**

> “I understood it immediately.”

**Finding:**  
The item-level calculation was understood by all four participants.

### T2q2. Was there a moment you wanted to say “that’s yours” to someone?

**P1**

> “Yes, with the protein.”

**P2**

> “Probably the protein because usually only one person uses something like that.”

**P3**

**P4**

**Finding:**  
Participants liked item-level assignment but identified a problem with assuming that the shopper is necessarily the item's owner.

### T2q3. Did passing feel like accusing, opting out, or neither?

**P1**

**P2**

> “It didn't feel aggressive.”

**P3**

**P4**

> “The button itself doesn't feel accusing, but because we're all sitting here, people still know what you're doing.”

**Finding:**  
Claim/Pass reduced the language of accusation. However, physical co-presence still created social visibility.

## T4 — Fair or Veto

|---|---|
| P1 | Veto |
| P2 | Looks Fair |
| P3 | Veto |
| P4 | Looks Fair |

A veto was therefore triggered.

### T4q1. Why did you choose Fair or Veto?

**P1**

> “I wanted to check the personal items again before locking it.”

**P2**

> “It looked close enough. I wouldn't normally argue about a few dollars.”

**P3**

> “I vetoed because our actual arrangement is more complicated than four individuals splitting everything.”

**P4**

> “I chose fair because I didn't want to keep adjusting a small grocery bill.”

**Finding:**  
Two distinct user priorities appeared:

- P1 and P3 prioritised precision and fairness.
- P2 and P4 prioritised speed and social convenience.

### T4q2. Do you believe the others cannot see who vetoed? What would prove it to you?

**P1**

> “I believe the app doesn't show it, but people can probably guess.”

**P2**

**P4**

> “I trust that the app doesn't show it. I just don't think it can stop people guessing.”

**Finding:**  
Participants understood technical anonymity but did not consider it equivalent to social anonymity.

### T4q3. Did you want to know who triggered it?

Immediately after the veto appeared, P2 looked towards P1 and said jokingly:

> “I think I know who did that.”

P4 laughed and looked between P1 and P3.

No participant explicitly asked the facilitator to reveal the voter, but participants attempted to infer the identity socially.

**Critical incident:**  
The design technically preserved anonymity, but group behaviour partially undermined C7.

**Tag:** `anonymity`

## T5 — Review

Because a veto occurred, participants entered the review process.

They reviewed expensive or disputed items.

### T5q1. “Personal” assigns the protein to whoever put it in the cart. Is that fair to the shopper?

**P1**

> “Only if they bought it for themselves.”

**P2**

> “Not always. I could buy something for P1.”

**P3**

**P4**

> “It should let us select whose item it actually is.”

**Strong finding:**  
All four participants independently identified the same issue.

The prototype should distinguish:

- who physically purchased the item; and
- who is financially responsible for the item.

### T5q2. Did the pizza slider match how your house actually eats?

**P1**

> “Not really. We normally wouldn't calculate exactly how much pizza each person ate.”

**P2**

**P4**

> “We would probably just split the pizza equally, or maybe split it between the two couples.”

**Finding:**  
The slider provided flexibility but required a level of consumption estimation that participants did not normally perform.

### T5q3. Did the result toast explain the new split well enough?

**P1**

**P2**

**P3**

> “I'd like an old amount and new amount comparison.”

**P4**

> “I mostly want to know the final payment.”

**Finding:**  
Participants understood individual changes but wanted stronger visibility of the transition between the original and revised totals.

## 11. Qualitative Tags

### `anonymity`

Participants understood that the interface concealed the veto user, but attempted to infer identity through body language and previous comments.

### `spinner-cruel`

Participants rejected random financial responsibility, particularly for genuinely communal items.

### `spinner-ok`

The animation itself was perceived as playful and entertaining before money became consequential.

### `bounty-price`

Participants felt that attaching fixed monetary values to chores could generate a second dispute.

### `maths`

Participants generally understood item-level splits but some needed additional time to connect the item breakdown with the final transfer.

### `shopper-unfair`

All participants challenged the assumption that the person putting an item in the trolley should automatically be financially responsible for it.

### `ping-vs-table`

Participants distinguished between complicated and simple expenses. Collaborative settlement was preferred for disputed bills, while a basic payment request was preferred for routine transactions.

### `silence`

Participants described previous occasions when they accepted small unfair costs instead of raising them directly.

## 12. Findings Against Design Claims

### C1 — People will contest an item if they do not have to put their name on it

**Result: Partially Supported**

Two of four participants used the veto.

Participants also reported that Claim/Pass made disagreement feel less confrontational.

However, social visibility remained because participants were physically present together.

### C2 — The app can take the heat so a housemate does not have to accuse anyone

**Result: Partially Supported**

The structured interaction reduced the need for statements such as:

> “You should pay for this.”

However, the application did not completely remove interpersonal pressure.

Participants still watched each other's reactions and attempted to infer preferences.

### C3 — Item-level claim/pass is clearer than one house total

**Result: Supported**

This was the strongest element of the prototype.

All four participants understood item-level splitting.

P1 and P3 particularly valued being able to separate personal purchases from household purchases.

Q4 achieved the highest mean score: **4.0 / 5**.

### C4 — After lock, people can explain why they owe a number

**Result: Mostly Supported**

Three of four participants could clearly identify at least one item-level line explaining their total.

P4 understood the required payment but had weaker understanding of the full calculation.

The final breakdown should therefore be simplified and make the connection between item decisions and final transfer more visible.

### C5 — A spinner feels like chance rather than a personal attack unless the amount is too high

**Result: Not Supported for Financial Assignment**

Participants found the wheel entertaining but did not consider randomness a legitimate basis for financial responsibility.

Even at $14.50, several participants rejected the result.

They strongly rejected using the mechanism for an $80 expense.

### C6 — A chore-for-cash trade is acceptable only if the house can reject it

**Result: Weak Support**

Participants appreciated that acceptance was required, but most would not use the mechanism in their household.

Their main concern was that assigning dollar values to housework creates another fairness negotiation.

### C7 — Nobody asks who played the veto

**Result: Not Supported**

Participants did not receive the identity through the interface, but they attempted to infer it through social behaviour.

P2 directly joked about who he thought used the veto.

In Mode B, this constitutes a weakness in C7 because the group still focused on identifying the voter.

---

## 13. Key Findings

### Finding 1 — Item-Level Splitting Is Valuable, but Not for Every Item

Participants strongly valued separating obvious personal purchases from shared purchases.

However, they did not want to individually vote on routine shared food every week.

**Design implication:**  
Introduce a faster mechanism such as **Mark as shared by everyone** before requiring individual Claim/Pass decisions.

### Finding 2 — Shopper and User Are Not the Same Role

The prototype currently risks interpreting the person who puts an item in the trolley as the owner.

All four participants rejected this assumption.

In this household, people frequently purchase products for their romantic partner.

**Design implication:**  
Allow users to select:

**Personal → Assign to P1 / P2 / P3 / P4**

rather than automatically assigning the product to the shopper.

### Finding 3 — Household Fairness Can Exist Across Time

The household does not judge fairness only from a single receipt.

One couple may spend more today because the other couple will buy and cook tomorrow.

**Design implication:**  
Consider supporting:

- weekly history;
- balance carried across multiple shopping trips;
- “Couple A paid last time” information;
- longer-term contribution visibility.

### Finding 4 — Couples Can Function as Financial Units

Although four individuals live in the house, some financial decisions are made at the couple level.

Participants sometimes think in terms of:

**Couple A vs Couple B**

rather than:

**P1 vs P2 vs P3 vs P4**

**Design implication:**  
Allow flexible splitting structures, for example:

- individual split;
- couple split;
- whole-house split.

### Finding 5 — Technical Anonymity Does Not Guarantee Social Anonymity

The interface successfully avoided displaying the veto user's name.

However, people sitting together could still infer identity based on:

- hesitation;
- body language;
- previous comments;
- known spending preferences.

**Design implication:**  
The design should not claim complete social anonymity.

Future iterations could explore allowing private voting before the group review begins or collecting all votes simultaneously.

### Finding 6 — The Spinner Is Playful but Financially Risky

Participants enjoyed the wheel as a visual interaction.

They rejected its authority to decide who pays for a shared expense.

**Design implication:**  
The spinner could instead determine:

- who does a small shared chore;
- who chooses the next cooking day;
- who initiates the next discussion;

rather than randomly allocating monetary responsibility.

Alternatively, orphan items could return to the group with:

- **Split equally**
- **Assign manually**
- **Re-deal**

### Finding 7 — Chore Pricing Risks Creating a Second Conflict

Participants did not reject chore exchange completely.

They rejected predefined financial values for chores.

**Design implication:**  
Replace:

**“Wash dishes — $10”**

with:

**“Offer to wash dishes instead — Accept / Reject”**

without assigning a fixed dollar value before the group agrees.

### Finding 8 — Different Bills Need Different Levels of Interaction

Participants repeatedly distinguished between simple and complicated expenses.

#### Simple bills

Example: four people sharing ordinary takeaway.

Preferred method:

> “Just tell me how much I need to transfer.”

#### Complicated bills

Example: groceries containing personal products and disputed items.

Preferred method:

> Review the individual items before finalising payment.

**Design implication:**  
Introduce two settlement modes:

- **Quick Split** for obvious shared expenses.
- **Review Together** for mixed or disputed expenses.

---

## 14. Overall Evaluation

The testing session suggests that The Weekly Split successfully addresses an important weakness of conventional equal bill splitting: the difficulty of separating personal and genuinely shared purchases without forcing one housemate to directly accuse another person.

The strongest feature was the **item-level Claim / Pass interaction**, particularly for expenses such as personal food, specialty products, or other goods that are not used equally.

However, the session also showed that fairness in a real household is more complex than a single receipt.

For these participants, fairness is negotiated across:

- individuals;
- couples;
- repeated meals;
- previous purchases;
- future responsibilities; and
- existing household routines.

The prototype therefore risks becoming too detailed if every grocery item must be negotiated independently.

The most significant weaknesses were:

1. low trust in social anonymity around the veto;
2. rejection of random financial assignment through the spinner;
3. low interest in fixed-price chore trading;
4. lack of support for couple-level expense sharing;
5. lack of multi-day expense history;
6. confusion between the shopper and the actual item user.

---

## 15. Recommended Changes for the Next Prototype

Based on this session, the next iteration should prioritise the following changes:

1. **Add “Shared by Everyone” as a fast option** for obvious communal products.
2. **Allow personal items to be manually assigned to another housemate**, rather than automatically assigning them to the shopper.
3. **Add optional couple/group splitting**, allowing expenses to be shared between Couple A and Couple B.
4. **Introduce expense history or carried balances** so fairness can be evaluated across several days rather than from one receipt.
5. **Replace random payment assignment for orphan items** with:
   - split equally;
   - assign manually;
   - re-deal;
   - or group discussion.
6. **Remove or reduce fixed dollar values from bounty cards** and allow the group to negotiate chore trades instead.
7. **Improve the locked-bill explanation** by showing:
   - previous total;
   - adjustments;
   - final total;
   - who pays whom.
8. **Consider a Quick Split mode** for straightforward bills and retain the full ritual for more complex or disputed expenses.

---

## 16. Conclusion

This Mode B session demonstrated that the prototype can create a structured and less confrontational way for housemates to discuss unequal expenses.

Participants generally understood the Claim/Pass system and considered item-level splitting useful, particularly where individual products were mixed into shared grocery purchases.

However, the testing also demonstrated that anonymity, randomness, labour exchange, and long-term household fairness require further refinement.

The most important insight from this household was that:

> **Fairness is not always achieved by making every individual receipt perfectly equal.**

For these participants, fairness often develops over time through alternating responsibility for groceries, cooking, and shared meals.

The next design iteration should therefore retain the transparency of item-level splitting while allowing more flexible household structures and faster settlement for routine shared expenses.

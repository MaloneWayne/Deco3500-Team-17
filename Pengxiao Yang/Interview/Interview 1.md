# Interview 1

**Participant profile:** Postgraduate student living in a shared student house.

## Question 1: The "Swallowed Resentment" Scenario (Testing the Anonymous Veto)

Tell me about a specific time when a shared house bill (like a grocery run or group takeout) included items you didn't consume or barely used, but you ended up paying an equal share anyway. Why did you choose not to speak up at the time, and how did that decision affect the dynamic in the house afterward?

**Answer**

Last month, my housemates ordered a huge group delivery that included expensive drinks and meat products I don't eat, but the total bill was just split equally among us on Splitwise. I kept quiet because I didn't want to come across as petty or disrupt the friendly group vibe over fifteen dollars. However, it created a subtle boundary after that, making me feel hesitant to join future group food orders altogether.

## Question 2: The Settlement Ritual (Testing Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Colocation)

Walk me through the exact routine your house currently uses to calculate and request money for shared expenses. Do you ever sit in the same room when the math is being done and the requests are sent, or does it happen entirely over text/push notifications? Where does that current routine feel the most awkward or stressful?

**Answer**

Our current routine is completely asynchronous; whoever pays just snaps a photo of the receipt and sends a payment request on WhatsApp late at night. We rarely sit together to calculate expenses because discussing money face-to-face feels naturally awkward and formal in a living room. The most stressful moment is getting an unexpected push notification for a large debt when you are in the middle of a study session.

## Question 3: The Unequal Share Dispute (Testing the "Slider of Fairness" / Blind Voting)

Think of a time when your house bought something to share (like a bulk grocery order, a large takeout meal, or shared drinks) but people consumed vastly different amounts. How did the group decide whether to split the cost evenly or proportionally, and how did you navigate the conversation if someone felt they were paying for more than they actually consumed?

**Answer**

We bought bulk household items and snacks recently, but two housemates consumed almost all the snacks within three days while the rest of us barely touched them. We tried to negotiate a proportional split over group chat, but estimating exact consumption felt subjective and quickly led to passive-aggressive replies. In the end, we just split it equally to end the argument, but everyone left the conversation feeling slightly cheated.

## Question 4: Non-Monetary Trades (Testing the Chore Barter/Bounty System)

Can you describe a situation where someone in the house was short on cash for a shared expense, so they compensated by taking on extra household duties (like buying the next round, doing a deep clean, or cooking)? How did the house negotiate and agree on the value of that favor?

**Answer**

During exam season, one of our housemates was tight on money for the monthly utility bill, so he offered to do all the kitchen deep cleaning and trash runs for two weeks instead. We verbally agreed to it because everyone was stressed with assignments and genuinely valued having clean shared spaces. It worked really well because it replaced financial pressure with practical empathy and domestic support.

## Question 5: Diffusing Tension (Testing Gamification / The App as the "Bad Guy")

Describe a time when a tense conversation about household money, chores, or responsibilities was successfully diffused by humor, a quick game (like rock-paper-scissors or a coin flip), or an external mediator. How did shifting the focus away from a direct argument change the mood in the room?

**Answer**

No one wanted to pay for the extra fee when our garbage overflowed, so we decided to settle who handles the chore and fee with a quick round of Mario Kart. Turning a potential conflict into a silly mini-game immediately broke the tension and brought laughter back into the room. It made the outcome feel like bad luck in a game rather than a personal disagreement between friends.

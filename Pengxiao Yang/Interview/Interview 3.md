# Interview 3

**Participant profile:** PhD candidate (Year 2) sharing a townhouse with two lab mates.

## Question 1: The "Swallowed Resentment" Scenario (Testing the Anonymous Veto)

Tell me about a specific time when a shared house bill (like a grocery run or group takeout) included items you didn't consume or barely used, but you ended up paying an equal share anyway. Why did you choose not to speak up at the time, and how did that decision affect the dynamic in the house afterward?

**Answer**

My housemates bought premium organic cooking oils and spices for a shared dinner, but I barely cook at home due to long lab hours. I paid my full split without complaining because I didn't want to spend my limited energy negotiating over small receipts. It made me establish a strict personal rule afterward to buy all my own groceries separately to avoid future overlaps.

## Question 2: The Settlement Ritual (Testing Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Colocation)

Walk me through the exact routine your house currently uses to calculate and request money for shared expenses. Do you ever sit in the same room when the math is being done and the requests are sent, or does it happen entirely over text/push notifications? Where does that current routine feel the most awkward or stressful?

**Answer**

We use a shared Google Sheet synced with a bank transfer log, updating it whenever utility or Internet bills arrive. Because of our conflicting experiment schedules, we handle payments 100% asynchronously without sitting down together. The most awkward part is when someone forgets to log a payment, leaving the balance sheet mismatched for weeks until someone notices.

## Question 3: The Unequal Share Dispute (Testing the "Slider of Fairness" / Blind Voting)

Think of a time when your house bought something to share (like a bulk grocery order, a large takeout meal, or shared drinks) but people consumed vastly different amounts. How did the group decide whether to split the cost evenly or proportionally, and how did you navigate the conversation if someone felt they were paying for more than they actually consumed?

**Answer**

We ordered a bulk delivery of printer paper and home office supplies, but one roommate used almost all of it for his heavy course printouts. We had an open discussion about it, and he willingly offered to cover 70% of the bill based on his usage. Having clear data on consumption made the conversation completely objective and stress-free for everyone involved.

## Question 4: Non-Monetary Trades (Testing the Chore Barter/Bounty System)

Can you describe a situation where someone in the house was short on cash for a shared expense, so they compensated by taking on extra household duties (like buying the next round, doing a deep clean, or cooking)? How did the house negotiate and agree on the value of that favor?

**Answer**

When one housemate was short on cash before his stipend arrived, he offered to handle all garden lawn mowing and waste management for a month. We calculated the monetary equivalent based on what a commercial mowing service would charge and offset his utility share accordingly. It was a win-win exchange because it saved us time while helping him manage his temporary cash flow issue.

## Question 5: Diffusing Tension (Testing Gamification / The App as the "Bad Guy")

Describe a time when a tense conversation about household money, chores, or responsibilities was successfully diffused by humor, a quick game (like rock-paper-scissors or a coin flip), or an external mediator. How did shifting the focus away from a direct argument change the mood in the room?

**Answer**

We were debating who should take on the hassle of calling the Internet provider to fix a billing error, so we used an online randomizer wheel to assign the task. Letting a neutral digital tool make the decision took away any perception of unfairness or personal bias. It immediately defused the reluctance, turning a tedious chore into a lighthearted group moment.

# Interview 2

**Participant profile:** Undergraduate student (Year 3) living in a 4-bedroom shared apartment near campus.

## Question 1: The "Swallowed Resentment" Scenario (Testing the Anonymous Veto)

Tell me about a specific time when a shared house bill (like a grocery run or group takeout) included items you didn't consume or barely used, but you ended up paying an equal share anyway. Why did you choose not to speak up at the time, and how did that decision affect the dynamic in the house afterward?

**Answer**

My roomies bought a high-end coffee machine and brand-name beans for the kitchen, but I only drink tea and never used it once. I decided not to raise an objection because they were all super excited about it, and I didn't want to ruin the house mood over money. It made me feel like an outsider in my own home for a few weeks, and I started spending more time studying at the library instead.

## Question 2: The Settlement Ritual (Testing Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Colocation)

Walk me through the exact routine your house currently uses to calculate and request money for shared expenses. Do you ever sit in the same room when the math is being done and the requests are sent, or does it happen entirely over text/push notifications? Where does that current routine feel the most awkward or stressful?

**Answer**

We handle everything asynchronously through Splitwise notifications, where one person uploads the bills at the end of every month. We never talk about it face-to-face because everyone has different class schedules and it feels too confrontation-heavy to bring up in person. The most awkward part is sending follow-up reminder ping messages when someone ignores the app notification for over a week.

## Question 3: The Unequal Share Dispute (Testing the "Slider of Fairness" / Blind Voting)

Think of a time when your house bought something to share (like a bulk grocery order, a large takeout meal, or shared drinks) but people consumed vastly different amounts. How did the group decide whether to split the cost evenly or proportionally, and how did you navigate the conversation if someone felt they were paying for more than they actually consumed?

**Answer**

We hosted a weekend house party and bought beer and snacks in bulk, but two people brought external guests who drank most of it. When we brought up splitting the cost based on guest counts, it triggered a heated debate on our group chat about what counts as "fair hospitality". In the end, we had to compromise on a rough 60/40 split, but it left a bitter taste because the rules weren't clear from the start.

## Question 4: Non-Monetary Trades (Testing the Chore Barter/Bounty System)

Can you describe a situation where someone in the house was short on cash for a shared expense, so they compensated by taking on extra household duties (like buying the next round, doing a deep clean, or cooking)? How did the house negotiate and agree on the value of that favor?

**Answer**

One housemate was waiting on his student allowance and couldn't pay his share of the monthly Wi-Fi and power bill on time. He offered to assemble all our new IKEA furniture and clean the balcony in exchange for a one-week payment delay. We all agreed instantly on our group chat because saving us hours of manual labor felt like a fair trade for a short delay.

## Question 5: Diffusing Tension (Testing Gamification / The App as the "Bad Guy")

Describe a time when a tense conversation about household money, chores, or responsibilities was successfully diffused by humor, a quick game (like rock-paper-scissors or a coin flip), or an external mediator. How did shifting the focus away from a direct argument change the mood in the room?

**Answer**

We had an ongoing dispute about who should pay the extra trash disposal fee after moving in, so we used a random wheel-spinner app to decide. Watching the wheel spin together turned an annoying argument into a funny, high-stakes moment for the whole house. Because the "app made the choice," the person who lost accepted the result without holding any personal grudges.

# Analysis

Pengxiao Yang · Team 17 · The Weekly Split  
Source: [Interview 1](Interview%201.md), [Interview 2](Interview%202.md), [Interview 3](Interview%203.md), [Interview 4](Interview%204.md)

Four people. Same five questions. All of them live with housemates. One postgraduate, one third-year undergraduate, one second-year PhD, one first-year undergraduate.

This is recall, not a kitchen observation. It is enough to see a pattern. It is not enough to freeze the design.

## What we asked, and why

| Prompt | What it tests in the product |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Paying for something you did not use, then staying quiet | Anonymous veto |
| 2. How the house actually settles | Same-room ritual vs async pings |
| 3. Unequal consumption | Claim / pass, fairness slider, blind review |
| 4. Chores instead of cash | Bounty cards |
| 5. Humour, games, or a tool taking the blame | Orphan spinner; the app as the bad guy |

## Participants at a glance

| | House | How they settle now | What silence cost them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I1 | Shared student house | WhatsApp receipt photo, late at night | Stopped joining group food orders |
| I2 | 4-bedroom apartment | Splitwise, once a month | Felt like an outsider; studied at the library |
| I3 | Townhouse with two lab mates | Google Sheet + bank log | Started buying groceries alone |
| I4 | Student housing, 3 roommates | Venmo / stories, on the spot | A few days of anxiety around group buys |

None of them sit in the same room to do the maths. That is not a gap in one house. That is the current ritual.

---

## Finding 1 — People swallow the bill, then leave the shared life

All four paid for items they did not use.

I1 ate none of the expensive drinks or meat, about fifteen dollars, and stayed quiet so they would not look petty. After that they hesitated to join later group orders. I2 never used the house coffee machine, did not want to kill the mood, and started working at the library. I3 barely cooks, paid for premium oils anyway, and then made a private rule: buy food separately. I4, new to the house, paid for energy drinks and alcohol they did not drink because they were “terrified of looking uncool.”

The dollar amount is small. The social move is large. Silence does not keep the house together. It pushes people out of the fridge, the order, or the room.

**Implication.** The veto cannot wait until someone is brave. If the only way to object is a speech, I1–I4 will not make it. The product has to object for them.

## Finding 2 — Settlement already happens on phones. The pain is the ping.

I1 gets a WhatsApp request in the middle of a study session. I2 has to nag people who ignore Splitwise for a week. I3’s sheet drifts for weeks when someone forgets a row. I4 gets a direct message for three dollars they forgot.

Face-to-face money talk feels “awkward and formal” (I1) or “too confrontation-heavy” (I2). Schedules do not line up (I2, I3). I4’s house does not even have a monthly method yet.

So: async tools are not missing. They are the whole system. They record a number after someone else has already decided what is fair. They also arrive at the worst time.

**Implication.** A colocated Sunday ritual is a change, not a polish. It has to feel shorter and less like a meeting than a living-room confrontation, or these houses will stay on WhatsApp. The app has to carry the rude question so sitting together is possible.

## Finding 3 — Equal split is the exit from a fight, not a fair answer

When consumption was uneven, three houses tried to talk. Two of them lost.

I1 tried a proportional split in group chat. Estimating who ate the snacks felt subjective. Replies turned passive-aggressive. They went back to equal. “Everyone left the conversation feeling slightly cheated.”

I2 argued about party guests and “fair hospitality.” They landed on a rough 60/40. The bitterness was the missing rule at the start, not the final ratio.

I4 used a joke, then a Snapchat poll, so nobody had to accuse anyone in the living room.

I3 is the counter-example. Printer paper had a visible usage story. The heavy user offered 70%. “Clear data… made the conversation completely objective.”

**Implication.** Item-level claim is not extra UI. It is the data I3 already needed. A slider or poll is for the grey cases (snacks, pizza, guests) where “personal vs communal” is the wrong binary. Chat is a bad room for this. A shared, simultaneous pass/claim is a better one.

## Finding 4 — Chore trades already work. Pricing them is optional, not required.

All four houses have swapped labour for money.

I1: two weeks of kitchen clean and bins during exams, agreed verbally, no rate. I2: IKEA plus balcony for a one-week delay on Wi-Fi and power, agreed in chat because the hours felt obvious. I4: a week of trash and dishes for kitchen supplies, agreed on Snapchat because nobody wanted the chores. I3: lawn mowing priced against a commercial service and offset against utilities — the only house that put a dollar on the job, and they called it a win-win.

This cuts both ways. Informal trades are already trusted. A hidden hourly rate is not what I1 or I4 asked for. A visible offset *can* help a house like I3.

**Implication.** Bounty cards should stay a proposal the others accept or reject. Do not auto-price a chore in the background. If the house wants a number, let them name it.

## Finding 5 — They already let a game, a wheel, or an app take the blame

I1 settled an overflow-bin fee with Mario Kart. Losing felt like “bad luck in a game,” not a fight. I2 used a spinner app for a trash fee; “the app made the choice,” so nobody held a grudge. I3 used an online randomizer to pick who calls the internet provider. I4 used rock-paper-scissors for three days of dirty dishes.

This is not us inventing fun. These houses already move the decision onto an object so a person does not have to be the villain.

**Implication.** The orphan spinner is native, not decorative. Keep it for leftovers and chores nobody wants. Do not spin a $150 grocery total. I2’s line is the design brief: the app made the choice.

## Cross-cutting needs

Drawn only from these four sessions:

1. **Object without naming a person.** I1 “petty,” I2 “ruin the mood,” I4 “uncool.”
2. **Show the line items, not only a total.** I3 got calm once usage was visible. I1 and I2 did not.
3. **More than one split method.** Equal, item, personal, and a middle state for pizza and snacks.
4. **A rule before the party, not after.** I2’s 60/40 hurt because hospitality was undefined.
5. **Labour as a visible option, group yes required.**
6. **A mediator that can be blamed.** Wheel, poll, game, or the product.

These line up with the team’s R1–R8. What these four add is the *cost of silence*: people do not only overpay. They opt out of shared meals, shared shops, and shared space.

## What this sample cannot claim

Four interviews. All of them study. None of this is a live Sunday with a real Woolies receipt. I4’s stack (Venmo, Snapchat, BeReal) may not be a Brisbane house. I3 already talks when the data is clear; not every house wants a spinner.

We did not ask “would you use The Weekly Split?” On purpose. The evidence is what they already do, and where it fails.

## One sentence

The maths is already on their phones. What is missing is a way to say “that is not mine” without becoming the difficult housemate — and a way to settle before the ping arrives in a study session.
